# SOP #9 — Cabinetry Veneer Polishing

**Service:** Interior Cabinetry & Tabletop Veneer Polishing  
**Author:** Shiny Jets  
**Source PDF:** `SOP-09-Cabinetry-Veneer.pdf`

_Shop standard operating procedure for Detailing AI. Prefer this over generic advice when diagnosing or quoting this service._

## 1. Purpose

Jet cabinetry — tabletops, side ledges, credenzas, bulkhead panels — is typically wood veneer over a substrate, bonded with glue, and finished with roughly seven layers of clearcoat. The clearcoat is what gets polished; the veneer and glue underneath are heat-sensitive.

Heat is the controlling constraint. If the surface gets too hot during polishing, the glue can soften and the veneer can lift, bubble, or delaminate. A burned-through clearcoat can be re-coated by a refinisher; a delaminated veneer means replacement.

The goal is to remove defects from the clearcoat while keeping the surface cool. No rotary polishers — DA only. Continuous thermal monitoring. Patience between passes.

## 2. Decision Matrix — When to Compound vs Polish Only

Surface Condition                                  Approach

Light swirl marks, minor haze, dull finish         Polish only — Fly Shiny Pro Polish + foam pad on DA

Scratches, etched water spots, deeper              Compound then polish — ACA 500 + wool pad on DA, then marring, oxidation                                 Polish Pro + foam pad on DA

Polish-only is the default. Step up to compound only when the defect requires it. The fewer passes the better — every pass is heat going into the surface.

## 4. Tools & Equipment

Polishing

- Rupes 3" battery-powered buffer — primary tool for veneer polishing. Battery-powered for cordless work inside the cabin.
- Mini Rupes Nano iBrid — for tight areas the 3" can't reach.
- DA only. No rotary on veneer under any circumstances.
- Wool pad (DA-specific) — for ACA 500 compounding.
- Foam pad — for Polish Pro.
- Pad-cleaning tool — Rag Company Tornador-style, straight air only. Clean pads outside the aircraft — air blow inside the cabin scatters debris onto leather, carpet, and panels.

Heat monitoring

- Thermal imaging camera on a tripod — primary monitoring. Aimed at the work area, watches surface temperature continuously while polishing.
- Laser-pointer infrared thermometer — fallback when a thermal camera isn't available. Spot-checks only — won't show heat spread the way the thermal camera does.

Lighting

- Portable lighting — aircraft interiors throw a lot of shadows from sidewalls, overhead panels, and the cabin curvature. Bring in enough light to actually see what you're polishing and inspecting.

Towels

- Autofiber Motherfluffer — primary wipe-off towel for compound and polish.
- Multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber — for final protection product wipe-on/buff-off and general utility.

### Other

- Mobile device with Shiny Jets CRM access
- Plastic sheeting for masking the entire surrounding area inside the cabin — sidewalls, seats, carpet, adjacent panels
- Painter's tape for securing the plastic edges

## 5. PPE

- Eye protection: Safety glasses meeting ANSI Z87.1 or EN 166.
- Hand protection: Nitrile gloves meeting EN ISO 374-1, minimum 6 mil.
- Foot protection: Closed-toe non-marking work shoes meeting EN ISO 20345 or ASTM F2413.
- Respiratory: Compounding interior veneer is very low-dust (the ACA 500 choice is part of why), but in confined cabin spaces a particulate mask is appropriate if working long sessions.

### Walkaround

Inspect each surface under good portable light:

- Surface condition — light defects → polish only; deeper defects → compound first
- Veneer health — check edges and seams for any existing lifting, bubbling, or separation. Don't polish over compromised veneer — heat and pressure will make it worse. Document and notify the customer.
- Adjacent materials — leather armrests, carpet, fabric panels next to tables. Tape or cover as needed.

Cabinetry stays in the aircraft. Detailers do not remove or install cabinetry, tabletops, or any interior trim — that's mechanic work. Work the surfaces in place.

## 7. Surface Preparation

1. Confirm the surface is at ambient room temperature before polishing. A surface warmed by sun through the windows is already starting hot — pre-cool the cabin or move the aircraft to shade.

2. Pop out cup holders from the table. Removable inserts come out cleanly and protect them from getting compound or polish in the recesses.

3. Clean the surface — wipe with a multi-purpose microfiber to remove dust, fingerprints, and surface debris. Anything left on the surface will be ground into the polish during the first pass.

4. Plastic the entire surrounding area — sidewalls, seats, carpet, adjacent panels, anything within range of where compound or polish can fling off the spinning pad. Use plastic sheeting taped at the edges. The Rupes pads spin compound and polish further than expected in a confined cabin. Full plastic is the rule, not selective taping.

5. Set up the thermal imaging camera on the tripod — aim it at the work area so the temperature display is visible while you polish. The thermal camera is what tells you when to stop.

6. Pre-prime the pads lightly per product instructions.

7. Stage products and towels within reach. Motherfluffer on the bench, final-protection products staged but capped.

## 8. Procedure — Polish-Only (Light Defects)

This is the default path. Use it when defects are limited to light swirl marks, minor haze, or dull finish.

Per Section (work in small sections — roughly 1' × 1' / 30 × 30 cm)
1. Apply 3–5 pea-sized drops of Fly Shiny Pro Polish onto a clean foam pad on the DA.
2. Spread the polish across the section with the pad off.
3. Engage the DA at a low speed first to fully spread the product, then increase to one speed below top speed for working.
4. Light pressure. Let the DA's weight do the work — pressure reduces orbital motion and adds heat fast.
5. Cross-hatch passes — horizontal then vertical with about 50% overlap, arm tempo about 1 inch (2.5 cm) per second. Cross-hatch is for even polish, not streak prevention.
6. Monitor the thermal camera continuously. Stop the pass if surface temperature rises above the safe range (see Section 10).
7. Maximum 45 seconds per section. Polish loses effectiveness past that point — wipe off, re-apply if more cut is needed.
8. Wipe with the Autofiber Motherfluffer. Dense fibers and sewn-inside edges, same standard as exterior polish wipe-off.

9. Inspect under portable light — interior shadows hide defects, so move the light around. If the section is clear, move on. If defects remain, decide whether to repeat polish or step up to compound.

Between Sections
- Confirm the section is back at ambient before moving on — the thermal camera will show this clearly. Laser thermometer works as a fallback if no thermal camera is on hand.
- Clean the foam pad with the Rag Company Tornador-style cleaner, straight air only, every 2–3 sections.

## 9. Procedure — Compound + Polish (Defects Present)

Use when polish alone won't lift the defect: scratches, etched water spots, deeper marring, oxidation.

### Step 1 — Compound with ACA 500

ACA 500 is the compound of choice here because it's virtually dustless — important for working in a cabin where dust ends up on leather, carpet, and panels.

1. Apply ACA 500 directly onto the surface (not onto the wool pad). Applying to the pad cakes the wool fibers; applying to the surface keeps fibers separated and lets fresh product reach the surface each pass.
2. Wool pad on the DA. No rotary on veneer, period.
3. Spread with the pad off, then engage at low speed.
4. Working speed: one speed below top speed on the DA.
5. Light pressure, cross-hatch passes, arm tempo about 1 inch (2.5 cm) per second.
6. Monitor the thermal camera continuously — wool generates more heat than foam. This is the highest-risk step.
7. Maximum 45 seconds per section. Watch the temperature; stop earlier if it's climbing.
8. Wipe with the Autofiber Motherfluffer.
9. Confirm temperature on the thermal camera. Allow the surface to return to ambient before moving on or before any follow-up pass. (Laser thermometer is the fallback if no thermal camera.)

### Step 2 — Polish with Fly Shiny Pro Polish

Follows the compound to remove micro-marring left by the wool pad and bring the finish back to clarity.

1. Apply 3–5 pea-sized drops of Polish Pro onto a clean foam pad on the DA.
2. Same procedure as polish-only (Section 8): low spread, increase to working speed, light pressure, cross-hatch, 45 seconds max per section, thermal monitoring continuous.
3. Wipe with the Autofiber Motherfluffer.

### Pad Cleaning

- Wool pad — every section. Wool loads fast; an unclean wool pad cuts deeper holograms.
- Foam pad — every 2–3 sections.
- Rag Company Tornador-style cleaner, straight air only — no solution tank.
- Clean pads outside the aircraft. Air-blowing inside the cabin scatters spent compound and removed clearcoat across the leather, carpet, and panels you just plasticked.
- Do not rake the wool pad on the DA. Raking is for rotary wool pads; on a DA, compressed air through the Tornador-style cleaner is enough.

## 10. Heat Management — The Critical Constraint

Veneer glue softens when the substrate gets hot. Once the glue softens, the veneer can lift, bubble, or delaminate, and the table is done. The DA generates heat through pad friction; the thinner the clearcoat and the smaller the section, the faster heat builds up.

Rules
- DA only. No rotary. A rotary on veneer is asking for delamination — direct rotation generates heat too fast.
- Light pressure on the DA. Pressing harder spikes heat and reduces orbital action.
- Maximum 45 seconds per section per pass.
- Thermal camera on the tripod monitors continuously. Aim it where you're working. If no thermal camera is available, use the laser thermometer for between-pass spot checks — but the thermal camera is the standard.
- Move to a new section if the working area is heating up — give the first section time to cool before returning to it.
- Keep pads cleaned — a loaded pad creates more friction and more heat.

Stop Polishing If
- Surface temperature climbs above safe-for-glue range during a pass — stop immediately.
- The clearcoat is hot to the touch with the back of your hand.
- The pad feels hot — it's transferring heat to the surface.
- You see any sign of haze or change in the clearcoat appearance that wasn't there before.

When in doubt: stop, let it cool, come back.

## 11. Final Protection

After polishing is complete and the surface is wiped clean with the Motherfluffer:

1. Confirm the surface is at ambient temperature.
2. Wipe with a clean multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber to remove any remaining polish residue.
3. Do not spray any protection product directly onto the veneer. Application is towel-to-surface only. Always follow the directions on the bottle of the specific product.

Fly Shiny Static Guard Spray Wax:

1. Spray Static Guard onto a clean multi-purpose microfiber towel — not onto the veneer.
2. Wipe onto the surface in light overlapping passes.
3. Let it haze.
4. Buff off with a separate clean microfiber.

Fly Shiny Air Guard:

1. Apply Air Guard onto a damp multi-purpose microfiber.
2. Wipe onto the surface in light overlapping passes.
3. Wipe dry with a separate clean microfiber before the product evaporates. Don't let Air Guard dry on the veneer — wipe through immediately after application.

The protection layer adds a hydrophobic film over the polished clearcoat, helps shed fingerprints, and makes future cleanings easier.

## 12. Quality Control

Walk each polished surface under good portable light:

- No swirl marks, no holograms, no hazing
- No streaks from the wipe-off
- No protection-product residue at edges or in seams
- Surface uniform — no thin spots in the polish, no missed sections
- Adjacent leather, carpet, and panels free of compound or polish overspray
- All tools, pads, towels, drop cloths, lighting, and the tripod removed from the cabin

Critical Don'ts

- Don't use a rotary polisher on veneer. DA only. Direct rotation generates heat too fast and is the most common way to delaminate veneer.
- Don't polish without the thermal camera running. Heat is the controlling constraint and you can't watch it without the tool.
- Don't press hard. Pressure spikes heat and reduces orbital motion. Slow the arm down instead.
- Don't work a section longer than ~45 seconds per pass. Compounds and polishes lose effectiveness, and continued friction is pure heat input.
- Don't polish over visibly compromised veneer (lifting, bubbling, separation at edges). Stop and notify the customer.
- Don't apply ACA 500 to the wool pad — apply it to the surface. Compound on the pad cakes the wool fibers and cuts deeper holograms.
- Don't skip pad cleaning. Wool every section, foam every 2–3 sections, straight air only.
- Don't wipe polish off with a general microfiber. Autofiber Motherfluffer for polish wipe-off.
- Don't apply final protection on a warm surface — wait for ambient.
- Don't spray Static Guard, Air Guard, or any protection product directly onto the veneer. Spray onto the towel; wipe onto the surface. Follow the directions on the bottle.
- Don't clean pads inside the aircraft. The Tornador-style cleaner scatters spent compound and clearcoat across the leather, carpet, and panels you just plasticked. Step outside to clean pads.
- Don't rake a wool pad on the DA. Raking is for rotary wool pads.
- Don't skip the full plastic of the surrounding area. Compound and polish fling further from the spinning pad than expected in a confined cabin.
- Don't remove or install cabinetry, tabletops, or interior trim. That's mechanic work, not detailer work. All veneer polishing is done with the surfaces in place.
- Don't put solution in the Tornador-style pad cleaner. Straight air only.
