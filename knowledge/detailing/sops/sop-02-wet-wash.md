# SOP #2 — Wet Wash

**Service:** Wet Wash (Full Exterior)  
**Author:** Shiny Jets  
**Version:** 1.1 (draft)  
**Source PDF:** `SOP-02-Wet-Wash.pdf`

_Shop standard operating procedure for Detailing AI. Prefer this over generic advice when diagnosing or quoting this service._

## 1. Purpose

A wet wash uses pressurized water and aviation soap to remove dirt, salt, exhaust film, hydraulic residue, and contaminants that a dry wash cannot fully address. It is the preferred service for:

- Aircraft operating in salty/coastal environments
- Aircraft with heavy accumulation between services

- Aircraft prior to paint correction, polishing, ceramic coating, or any service requiring a fully decontaminated surface
- Aircraft after extended outdoor storage or weather exposure

Wet wash removes corrosive deposits (especially salt) that accelerate paint degradation and metal corrosion. It is performed outside or in an approved wash bay with drainage — never inside a hangar without containment.

A wet wash is completed before any other exterior service (polishing, ceramic, brightwork, de-ice boots, windows).

## 3. Tools & Equipment

- Pressure washer — minimum 4 GPM, 3,500 PSI
- Foam cannon attachment for pressure washer
- Spot-free water source (reverse osmosis or deionized) for final rinse
- IK Sprayer (pressurized) for applying chemicals to brushes/towels
- Soft-bristle brush for paint
- Heavy-duty brush for landing gear
- Wash mitt (microfiber or sheepskin) for hand-cleaning panels
- Air blower or leaf blower (optional, speeds drying and clears panel gaps)

- Creeper (for belly access)
- Ladder tall enough for specific aircraft
- Headlamp for inspection lighting
- Pitot tube covers
- Engine inlet covers / engine plugs (mandatory for jets and turboprops)
- Static port covers (or zero-residue painters tape — locate every static port)
- NACA duct / pitot-static probe / TAT probe covers as applicable
- Mobile device with Shiny Jets CRM access

### Towels (color-coded)

- Dreadnaught XL twist-pile microfiber (1100 GSM) — main wash drying
- Dreadnaught blue 16"×16" — windows only (isolated)
- Cotton terry towels — oil and exhaust soot wipe-off
- Multi-purpose microfiber — hydraulic wipe-down, utility

## 4. PPE

PPE must meet recognized international occupational safety standards.

- Eye protection: Safety glasses meeting ANSI Z87.1 or EN 166 (impact + splash rated). Full face shield recommended when operating pressure washer near the aircraft.
- Hand protection: Nitrile chemical-resistant gloves meeting EN ISO 374-1 and EN ISO 374-5. Disposable nitrile minimum 6 mil.
- Foot protection: Closed-toe, non-marking, slip-resistant work shoes meeting EN ISO 20345 or ASTM F2413
- Hearing protection (pressure washer + APU + adjacent aircraft): EN 352-1 or ANSI S3.19 rated
- Clothing: Long pants, long sleeves recommended. Waterproof apron or coveralls reduce soak-through.

### Walkaround

Conducted before unloading any equipment. Walk the aircraft in the same direction every time so it becomes muscle memory.

### Inspect and document

- Dents, scratches, cracks, corrosion — fuselage, wings, tail, engines
- Damaged or missing fasteners on access panels
- Hydraulic leaks, bent struts, damaged wheels/tires
- Cracks/chips on windows and windshields
- Antenna damage or corrosion
- Fading, peeling, chipping paint
- Excessive oil or fluid leaks (stop work and notify POC immediately)
- Open vents, ports, drains that need protection during wash
- Existing water intrusion or staining near doors, windows, antennas

### Confirm closed and latched

- All cabin doors
- Luggage doors
- All windows that open
- Fuel caps
- Service panels and ports
- Avionics bay doors

If damage is found: Stop. Notify pilot, owner, or mechanic (your designated POC) before starting work.

## 6. Aircraft Preparation

Water intrusion is the #1 risk of a wet wash. Every opening must be sealed before water touches the aircraft.

1. Install pitot tube covers.
2. Cover every static port. Some aircraft have multiple — confirm count with mechanic or maintenance manual if unsure.

3. Install engine inlet covers / plugs (mandatory for jets and turboprops). For piston engines, cover the air intake.
4. Cover NACA ducts, TAT probes, AOA sensors, and any other exposed sensors.
5. Cover antennas only if directed by the manufacturer or POC (most antennas tolerate water — covering can do more harm than good).
6. Confirm all doors, windows, and service panels are closed.
7. Walk a final perimeter check: any opening not built to take water spray must be covered.
8. Position pressure washer, foam cannon, IK sprayer, creeper, and ladder for the side you'll start on.
9. Set out towels in dedicated bins (clean / dirty / windows isolated).
10. Confirm the wash area has working drainage and is approved for aircraft wash water capture (environmental compliance).

## 7. Procedure — Piston Aircraft

Apply chemicals before water touches the aircraft. Once water is on the surface, chemical cleaners can't fully penetrate and the wash becomes less effective.

### Step 1 — Oil Removal

Spray Fly Shiny Oil Delete (oil remover) onto a folded cotton terry towel. Wipe oil off the belly and any oil-affected panels. You do not need to remove every trace — break the oil free so the Carbon Blitz pass (Step 2) and main wash can finish the job.

For Skydrol (hydraulic fluid): Spray Oil Delete on a terry towel, wipe the affected area, then wipe dry with a fresh multi-purpose microfiber towel — the surface is clean and shiny, no further treatment needed. Skydrol areas are exempt from Step 11 neutralization.

### Step 2 — Exhaust Soot Removal (after Oil Delete on Engine Oil Areas)

Spray Fly Shiny Carbon Blitz (soot/carbon remover) onto a folded terry towel. Wipe soot-affected panels — typically belly and aft of exhaust stacks. Re-apply Carbon Blitz to the towel as it loses effectiveness.

Why this step follows Oil Delete on piston aircraft: Oil Delete removes oil but does not remove exhaust soot. On piston aircraft, oil and exhaust soot are co-located, so the same area cleaned with Oil Delete must also be cleaned with Carbon Blitz to fully remove residue.

Critical: Do not let Carbon Blitz dry on the paint. Etching is permanent. If a panel is exposed to direct sun and dries too fast, work in smaller sections.

### Step 3 — Landing Gear and Flaps (Low-Wing Aircraft)

All aircraft: Pre-treat the landing gear. Apply landing gear cleaner with the IK Sprayer onto a heavy-duty brush, then agitate the gear. Avoid direct spray on the chrome strut — work the cleaner with the brush only. The wash and rinse will finish the gear.

Low-wing aircraft: Pre-treat flap leading edges. Heavy bug load should be loosened with Fly Shiny Bug Blitz (bug remover) since bugs in tight flap areas are hard to remove during the main rinse.

High-wing aircraft: Skip the flap step — bugs will come off during the main wash pass.

### Step 4 — Pre-Rinse

Starting from the top of the aircraft and working down, pre-rinse the entire airframe with the pressure washer.

- Hold the wand at least 2–3 feet (60–90 cm) from the surface for general panels
- Hold further back on composite panels, antennas, decals, leading edges, and registration markings
- Spray at a downward angle — never spray upward into panel gaps, door seams, or windows
- Avoid direct spray on pitot/static covers (water can defeat the cover seal)
- Loosen and rinse away loose contaminants before applying soap

### Step 5 — Foam Soap Application

Mix Fly Shiny Aviation Wash (aviation soap) in the foam cannon at the manufacturer-recommended ratio. Apply foam to the entire aircraft from bottom to top — counterintuitive, but this prevents soap streaks from running down over already-soaped panels (which would cause uneven dwell).

The thick foam layer should sit on the surface for 2–4 minutes of dwell — long enough for the soap to break contaminants free, short enough that it doesn't dry. If you're in direct sun and the foam starts drying, re-foam that section.

### Step 6 — Agitate Where Needed

For heavier contamination, agitate with a soft-bristle brush or wash mitt, working in straight lines (not circles) from top to bottom.

- Soft-bristle brush on painted panels
- Heavy-duty brush on landing gear and inside wheel wells
- Wash mitt on flat painted surfaces if no heavy contamination
- Never use the same brush on landing gear and paint — color-code or label

### Step 7 — Main Rinse (Top-Down)

Rinse the entire aircraft top-down with the pressure washer at the same 2–3 ft minimum distance. Watch for soap residue — any remaining foam means re-rinsing.

Special-surface rules during rinse:

- Painted leading edges: If bugs remain after the rinse, spray Bug Blitz over the leading edge, then re-foam and re-rinse the section.
- Polished aluminum leading edges: Do not use Bug Blitz. For stubborn bugs/stains, use Fly Shiny Maverick (sprayable metal polish) to remove bug etchings. Do not use Maverick on coated aluminum or on weeping edges.
- De-ice boots: Aviation Wash only — no other cleaners. Light pressure rinse. Watch for vortex generators (Citation Mustang and others) — bumping or blasting them can damage the entire boot.

### Step 8 — Spot-Free Final Rinse

Switch to spot-free water (reverse osmosis or deionized) for the final rinse. This removes any remaining minerals from the wash water and prevents water spots as the aircraft dries.

- Rinse top-down, the same direction as the main rinse
- Do not skip this step on a polished, ceramic-coated, or freshly painted aircraft
- Hard-water spots become permanent etching on polished aluminum and clear coats

### Step 9 — Dry

Dry the aircraft before the spot-free water can air-dry on its own.

- Use Dreadnaught XL twist-pile microfiber, top-down
- Wipe in straight lines, not circles
- Air blower / leaf blower speeds up drying on large areas and forces water out of panel gaps, hinges, and fastener heads — use it especially around doors, control surfaces, and gear bays
- Open both sides of the towel before tossing in dirty bin

### Step 10 — Windows (Final)

After the body is fully dry and overspray from rinse is over, return to windows.

1. Spray Aviation Wash diluted, or Fly Shiny Quick Turn (dry wash), evenly across each window.
2. Wipe with the dedicated blue 16"×16" (40×40 cm) Dreadnaught (windows only).
3. Wipe in the direction of airflow over the window.
4. Repeat until clear from all viewing angles.

The smaller dedicated towel reduces the risk of dragging contaminants like paint chips or PRC sealant from surrounding areas onto the glass/acrylic.

### Step 11 — Neutralize Chemical Treatment Areas (Engine Oil + Soot Areas Only)

Return to every panel where Oil Delete and Carbon Blitz were used together (engine oil + soot areas — belly and aft of exhaust). Spray Fly Shiny Quick Turn over the treated area and wipe clean with a fresh Dreadnaught XL.

Cleaner residue left on paint attracts dirt, slowly degrades the aircraft paint, and over time creates corrosion.

Skydrol/hydraulic areas are exempt — those were already finished with Oil Delete + dry microfiber in Step 1.

This step is not optional for engine oil + soot areas.

## 8. Procedure — Turboprop / Jet Aircraft

### Step 1 — Engine Oil

A dry cotton terry towel only is sufficient for engine oil on jets and turboprops. No chemical needed. Wipe oil-affected panels (typically around engine cowlings and aft of nacelles) with a dry terry towel before any other work.

### Step 2 — Hydraulic Oil (Skydrol)

For hydraulic oil leaks or Skydrol residue:

1. Spray Fly Shiny Oil Delete (oil remover) onto a folded cotton terry towel.
2. Wipe the hydraulic-affected area.
3. Wipe dry with a fresh multi-purpose microfiber towel — the surface is clean and shiny, no further treatment needed.

Skip this step if no hydraulic leaks are present.

### Step 3 — Landing Gear and Flaps (Low-Wing — Most Jets)

All jets/turboprops: Pre-treat the landing gear. Apply landing gear cleaner with the IK Sprayer onto a heavy-duty brush, then agitate. Avoid direct spray on the chrome strut.

Low-wing aircraft: Pre-treat flap leading edges with Bug Blitz if heavy bug load is present.

### Step 4 — Pre-Rinse, Foam, Agitate, Rinse

Follow Steps 4–7 from the piston procedure. On jets:

- Engine inlets must be covered before any water touches the aircraft — water ingestion into a turbine engine can cause severe damage and is an FAA-reportable event.
- Exhaust soot and bugs are removed during the main wash itself — no pre-treatment required for these under normal soot/bug loads.
- Heavy thrust reverser soot on painted panels (the exception): apply Carbon Blitz with a terry towel locally before the foam pass, work it in, and re-foam over the section so it doesn't dry. Rinse with the rest of the aircraft.

- Composite panels (radomes, fairings, control surface skins): hold the pressure wand further back and use lower angle. Composites are more easily damaged by direct high-pressure blast.

### Step 5 — Spot-Free Final Rinse, Dry, Windows

Follow Steps 8, 9, and 10 from the piston procedure.

## 9. Quality Control Walkaround

Walk the aircraft in the same direction as the pre-wash. Verify:

- No streaks, missed panels, or water pooling
- No soap residue in panel gaps, door seams, or fastener heads
- No towels, brushes, ladders, creeper, or tools left behind
- Pitot/static covers cleaned and reinstalled (or if tape was used, remove tape and wipe area clean)
- Engine inlet covers removed and stowed
- All sensor covers removed
- Windows clear from all viewing angles
- All applicable neutralization complete (engine oil + soot areas only; Skydrol/hydraulic areas already finished with Oil Delete + dry microfiber)
- Water has not intruded into any service panel, door seam, or avionics bay (open visible panels briefly to confirm dry)
- Hangar/wash bay floor cleared and drainage confirmed working
- Any covers/plugs cleaned and reinstalled

## 10. Critical Don'ts

- Don't start any service before the pre-wash CRM photo upload and walkaround.
- Don't leave the aircraft before the post-wash CRM upload is complete.
- Don't spray water at any opening, panel gap, door seam, or sensor at close range or upward angle.
- Don't wet-wash a jet or turboprop without engine inlets covered.
- Don't use silicone-containing soap — it ruins future paint adhesion, touch-up, and ceramic.
- Don't skip the spot-free water final rinse — water spots are permanent on polished and coated surfaces.
- Don't let any chemical cleaner (especially Carbon Blitz or degreaser) dry on the paint — etching is permanent.
- Don't skip the neutralization step on engine oil + soot areas (piston).
- Don't scrub windows with pressure — drag gently.
- Don't use Bug Blitz on polished aluminum brightwork or de-ice boots.
- Don't bump vortex generators on de-ice boots.
- Don't direct-spray the chrome strut on landing gear — you'll strip the seal lubrication.
- Don't use the same brush on landing gear and paint.
- Don't wet-wash inside a hangar without containment and approved drainage.
- Don't wet-wash in direct sun on a hot day — soap and cleaners dry too fast to work properly.
