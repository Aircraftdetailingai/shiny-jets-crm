# SOP #3 — Leather Cleaning & Conditioning

**Service:** Leather Cleaning, Stitching, Seat Belts, Embroidery, Suede, Conditioning  
**Author:** Shiny Jets  
**Source PDF:** `SOP-03-Leather-Cleaning.pdf`

_Shop standard operating procedure for Detailing AI. Prefer this over generic advice when diagnosing or quoting this service._

## 1. Purpose

Aircraft leather is significantly more delicate and costly than automotive leather. A single set of aircraft seats can run into the tens of thousands of dollars to replace, and all interior materials are flammability-tested and certified — meaning any damage to leather, stitching, or trim affects both aesthetics and certification. Leather cleaning is divided into three modes:
- Light leather cleaning — routine maintenance, no chemicals beyond water
- Standard leather cleaning — Scrub Ninja with Koch Chemie Polstar, panel by panel
- Stain-specific leather cleaning — Scrub Ninja with Colourlock Strong Leather Cleaner. Same tool and method as standard cleaning; used when Polstar cannot lift a stain (ink, dye transfer, deep set-in spills). Plus dedicated procedures for stitching, seat belts, embroidery, suede / micro suede / Alcantara, and leather conditioning. The leather cleaning service is performed after Interior Maintenance Cleaning (SOP #1). The cabin must already be vacuumed, dusted, and wiped down before leather work begins.

## 3. Tools & Equipment

### Cleaning tools

- Scrub Ninja — primary leather cleaning pad for both Polstar and Colourlock Strong. Densely packed soft fibers, high product-holding capacity, gets into crevices and wrinkles without abrading the leather.
- Horsehair brush — stitching, embroidery, suede / micro suede / Alcantara. Gentle enough not to fray stitching.
- Tornador (Black model with onboard chemical injection) — blasting stitching clean. The black model has a spinning metal nozzle inside the cone — keep it ~1" (2.5 cm) away from leather to avoid contact damage. Chemical injection is built into the gun; fill with Koch Chemie Polstar for stitching work.
- Dusting brush (soft, hand-held) — tabs, control centers, intricate recesses.
- MetroVac blower (from SOP #4 kit) — suede / micro suede / Alcantara drying. No temperature setting; the only heat is from the motor itself, which is mild and not enough to scorch fibers under normal use. Used moving, never parked.
- YINMIK Digital pH Meter (from SOP #4 kit) — required for seat belt cleaning. Baseline reading from a clean section, stain pH diagnosis, rinse water verification, post-rinse confirmation. The probe used directly on wet webbing.

### Vacuum

- Handheld cordless vacuum (Fanttik V8 APEX or equivalent) with crevice tool on the small hose. Used for pre-vacuuming each seat before any wet cleaning. The handheld is used here instead of the MetroVac because pre-vacuuming each seat is brief work between cleaning steps — running the MetroVac for hours on intermittent use is impractical without a remote switch. Towels
- Multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber — wiping up leather cleaner, buffing after conditioning. Folded into quarters; flip when one side loads.
- Cotton terry towels — general utility (oil dabbing, etc).
- Large Dreadnaught towel — laid over the cushion during seat belt cleaning. The seat belt lays on top of the Dreadnaught so dirty water and cleaner from the belt are absorbed by the Dreadnaught instead of transferring onto the cushion.

### Applicators

- Microfiber applicator pad — applying leather conditioner. Reusable.

### Protection

- Runner / clean drop cloth — laid in the cabin aisle to protect high-traffic carpet during leather work.
- 2" (5 cm) blue painter's tape — protecting hard surfaces from abrasion as the Tornador air hose drags across cabinetry, panel edges, and door frames during stitching work.
- Carpet fan or shop fan — placed at one end of the aircraft with both doors open. Critical when using the Tornador, since it disperses cleaner into the air; the fan replaces interior air with fresh air during and after the service.

### Other

- Headlamp — inspection and detail work
- Mobile device with Shiny Jets CRM access

## 4. PPE

PPE must meet recognized international occupational safety standards.
- Eye protection: Safety glasses meeting ANSI Z87.1 or EN 166. Required when operating the Tornador.
- Respirator: Required when operating the Tornador. Full-face coverage preferred. Cleaner is aerosolized fast in a poorly ventilated cabin. Aircraft doors must still be open with a fan running, regardless of respirator status.
- Hand protection: Nitrile chemical-resistant gloves meeting EN ISO 374-1 and EN ISO 374-5. Disposable nitrile minimum 6 mil. Fresh gloves whenever switching between leather, stitching, lav, or any other zone.
- Foot protection: Shoe covers required in the aircraft. Replace any time they pick up debris.
- Clothing: Clean uniform. No belt buckles, watches, rings, or hard accessories. Long pants and sleeves recommended when working with chemicals.

## 6. Aircraft Preparation

1. Confirm the detailer doing leather work has not polished brightwork that day. Metal polish residue stays on clothing, hands, gloves, and tools and can transfer to leather, fabric, headliners, and other interior surfaces — where it can permanently stain. This is not a minor cross-contamination risk. A single brightwork-contaminated detailer can ruin an entire aircraft interior. Either swap detailers or have the detailer fully change clothes and decontaminate (clean skin, fresh gloves, clean tools) before entering the cabin.
2. Confirm SOP #1 (Interior Maintenance Cleaning) is complete or being run together — leather work is done after the cabin is vacuumed and hard surfaces wiped.
3. Wipe down all tools (Tornador, hoses, bottles, applicators) outside the aircraft before bringing them in.
4. Place dedicated bins at the door for: clean towels, dirty towels.
5. Lay a runner or clean drop cloth in the aisle to protect carpet from foot traffic during leather work.
6. Tape any hard surface (cabinetry edges, panel corners, door frames) where the Tornador air hose will drag during use. The hose is the main abrasion risk during stitching work.
7. On low-wing aircraft, tape the trailing edge of the wing at the entry side to protect the paint from abrasion as the crew carries tools in and out.

8. Position the carpet fan at one end of the aircraft, with both entry and luggage doors open (when both are present). This is critical for the Tornador work — fresh air must continuously replace the cabin air to clear aerosolized cleaner.
9. Confirm cabin lighting is bright. If the aircraft is on ground power, use cabin lights. If ground power is not available, work by headlamp.

## 7. Each Seat — Pre-Vacuum (Before Any Wet Cleaning)

Every seat is dust-vacuumed before wet cleaning begins. Wet cleaning over crumbs and grit grinds them into the leather. This step is non-negotiable. For each seat, working top to bottom:
1. Headrest recess — the attachment point where the headrest plugs into the seat. Use the crevice tool. This area collects hidden dust and crumbs.
2. Headrest itself — soft brush + vacuum nearby (same dust-and-capture technique from SOP #1).
3. Cushion and crevices — the gap between the cushion and the seat back, between cushion and side bolsters. Crevice tool, working slowly and methodically.
4. Armrests and the gap between cushion and armrest — vacuum the recesses, seams, and gap between the cushion and the armrest. Some aircraft armrests slide up, others lift, and some are fixed — clean what's accessible from the gap regardless of how the armrest moves (or doesn't).
5. Tab recesses — the recess where the swivel-lock or recline tab sits.
6. Swivel the seat to access the side facing the aircraft wall, and vacuum that side.
7. Back pocket — remove any magazines, safety cards, or placards (photograph their position first). Vacuum out the pocket interior.
8. Return magazines and placards to their exact photographed position when finished. Compressed air (from the Tornador on air-only mode, or shop compressor) can supplement the vacuum in the deepest crevices — blast debris into the open, then vacuum it up.

## 8. Light Leather Cleaning (Routine Maintenance)

Use this method when:
- The leather is in good condition with no significant staining
- The job is routine maintenance, not restoration
- The customer requested light cleaning only

Procedure
1. Fold a multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber towel into quarters.
2. Dampen one side lightly with water only — no chemical cleaner. The towel should be damp, not wet.
3. Wipe the leather in gentle sections — armrests, cushion sides, seat back, headrest.
4. As the working side loads up with dirt, flip to a fresh quarter.
5. When all four quarters are loaded, swap to a fresh towel.

This method works because microfiber alone, slightly dampened, lifts light soil and skin oils from pigmented leather's clearcoat without any chemistry. If light cleaning does not get the leather clean, escalate to Section 9 (Standard) and quote accordingly. Do not oversaturate. Damp is correct. Wet is wrong.

## 9. Standard Leather Cleaning — Scrub Ninja + Koch Chemie Polstar

This is the primary leather cleaning method for most jobs.

Procedure For each seat, working panel by panel — never the whole seat at once, because cleaner left on leather to dry causes dirt to re-adhere:
1. Spray Koch Chemie Polstar onto the Scrub Ninja pad. The pad should be lightly saturated — moist enough to clean effectively, not so wet that it drips.
2. Scrub a single panel (e.g., one armrest, or the seat cushion, or the seat back) with the Scrub Ninja using moderate pressure. The dense fibers reach into wrinkles and crevices without needing to stretch the leather flat.
3. The cleaner will foam as you scrub. Wipe the foam and cleaner up before it dries or evaporates — if you let it dry, the dirt that was lifted by the foam settles back onto the leather surface and re-adheres. Wipe with a folded 16"×16" (40×40 cm) multi-purpose microfiber or cotton terry towel (either works). Flip to fresh quarters as the towel loads.
4. Inspect the panel under headlamp light. If soil remains, repeat the panel.
5. Move to the next panel and repeat. Swivel the seat during cleaning to reach the side facing the wall — do not skip this side.

Critical rules
- One panel at a time. Never apply cleaner to the entire seat and come back to wipe — the cleaner will dry and pull dirt back into the leather.
- Damp Scrub Ninja, not wet. Saturation should be enough to clean, not enough to drip.
- Patch-test first on any aircraft you have not cleaned before, in an inconspicuous spot, to confirm compatibility with the leather finish.
- Anything beyond a smudge that doesn't clean up in one or two passes with Polstar is either stain-specific territory (Section 10) or damaged leather — don't keep escalating pressure on standard cleaning.

## 10. Stain-Specific Cleaning — Scrub Ninja + Colourlock Strong Leather

Cleaner Some stains will not lift with Polstar — ink, dye transfer (especially blue from denim), and deeper set-in spills sometimes require stronger chemistry to break the bond. Colourlock Strong Leather Cleaner is virtually identical to Polstar in usage method; the only difference is the strength of the chemistry.

Same tool (Scrub Ninja), same panel-by-panel approach, same foam-and-wipe rule as Section 9.

Pre-conditions Before using Strong Leather Cleaner, verify:
- Standard cleaning with Polstar (Section 9) has been attempted on the panel and was insufficient
- The leather's clearcoat and dye are still intact (no raw leather exposed at the stain)
- Patch test passed in an inconspicuous spot — if color comes off into the towel during the patch test, stop. The leather has deteriorated and is not a candidate for cleaning. Re-dyeing or replacement is required.
- Customer has been informed when the stain is severe enough that deeper cleaning may still not fully remove it Note on high-traffic seats: The patch-test area may not reflect high-traffic conditions. High-use seats (pilot, co-pilot, primary passenger seat) experience clearcoat wear that inconspicuous areas have not. If a high-traffic area shows color transfer onto the towel during cleaning, stop immediately and notify the customer.

Procedure For each affected panel:
1. Spray Colourlock Strong Leather Cleaner onto the Scrub Ninja pad. Lightly saturated — not dripping.
2. Scrub the panel with moderate pressure. The cleaner will foam.
3. Wipe the foam and cleaner up before it dries or evaporates — same rule as Polstar. Dried cleaner lets lifted dirt re-adhere to the leather. Use a 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber or cotton terry towel, folded into quarters.
4. Inspect under headlamp. Stop if any color transfers to the towel.
5. Move to the next panel.

Mixed approach It is normal to use both products on the same seat:
- Polstar (Section 9) for most panels
- Colourlock Strong on one or two panels with stubborn stains
- Strong cleaner targeted at a specific stain (ink mark, dye transfer) on an otherwise Polstar-cleaned seat Choose the lighter product (Polstar) by default. Escalate to Strong only when Polstar can't get the stain.

## 11. Stitching Cleaning

Stitching cleaning is performed after leather cleaning is complete on each seat, because the leather cleaning may push dirt into the stitching.

Setup
1. Fan running at the cabin door, both aircraft doors open.
2. Respirator and safety glasses on — Tornador will disperse cleaner into the air.
3. Two multi-purpose microfiber towels at hand.
4. Tornador with Koch Chemie Polstar loaded in the onboard chemical injector.
5. Horsehair brush ready.
6. Bottle of Koch Chemie Polstar.

Procedure — One Run of Stitching at a Time
1. Spray Koch Chemie Polstar directly into the horsehair brush.
2. Lightly scrub one run of stitching. Light suds will form. Do not scrub aggressively — stitching frays under heavy pressure.
3. Wipe up the excess cleaner with a multi-purpose microfiber towel.
4. Tornador blast: Hold the Tornador cone with your finger as a guide so the nozzle stays approximately 1 inch (2.5 cm) away from the stitching (the spinning metal nozzle inside the cone can damage leather on contact). Wrap one of the microfiber towels around the muzzle of the Tornador to contain the spray. Blast the stitching using the Tornador's chemical injection feature with Koch Chemie Polstar.
5. Wipe up the solution with the second microfiber towel.
6. Switch the Tornador chemical injector to OFF (air-only mode). Blast the stitching with air until it is fully dry.
7. Move to the next run of stitching and repeat.

Embroidery Embroidery uses the same method as stitching with two adjustments:
- Pressure on the horsehair brush: light touch on embroidery is a reasonable default since the threads sit raised above the leather surface and are visually delicate, but in practice horsehair is gentle enough that the same pressure used on stitching usually works. Adjust to the specific embroidery you're cleaning.
- Hold the Tornador further from the surface (~2 inches (5 cm) instead of 1) — embroidery cannot tolerate the close-range blast that stitching can.

## 12. Seat Belt Cleaning

Seat belts collect skin oils, food, and stains. They are cleaned after stitching, because seat belt water and cleaner cannot be allowed to transfer onto freshly cleaned leather.

Regulatory Context Aircraft seat belts are TSO-certified (typically TSO-C22 / TSO-C22g) and must hold their rated tensile load. FAA AC 43.13-1B, Chapter 9 covers inspection and airworthiness standards for belts — what to look for to determine if a belt is unairworthy and must be replaced. It does not contain cleaning procedures.

Chemically contaminated webbing is one of the conditions that renders a belt unairworthy, which is why the cleaner choice matters. The cleaning method itself is informed by AmSafe's published Instructions for Continued Airworthiness:
- Webbing is cleaned with warm water and a mild household soap (dishwashing liquid or laundry detergent are explicitly listed by AmSafe as acceptable).
- No isopropyl alcohol. No dry cleaning. No immersion. No machine washing.
- Soap and water never on the metal parts — the buckle, latch, slide, and end fittings are cleaned separately with Quick Turn on a microfiber.
- The webbing is hand-cleaned with gentle scrubbing using a soft brush.
- Always check the aircraft's maintenance manual for any operator-specific approved cleaner list or restrictions.

Fire Retardant Treatment — Why pH Matters Aircraft seat belt webbing is fire-retardant-treated to meet FAR 25.853 (flammability standards for aircraft cabin interiors). The most common treatment on aviation polyester webbing is phosphate-phosphonate chemistry, applied at roughly 0.1-0.3% by weight. This treatment is what allows the belt to pass the vertical burn test required for cabin certification. The FR treatment is stable across pH 4-8 but degrades outside that range:
- Strong alkalinity (pH 9+) slowly hydrolyzes the phosphate esters, degrading the FR over the life of the belt.
- Strong acidity (below pH 4) can affect the dye over time but is less of a concern for FR.
- Heat plus alkalinity accelerates FR degradation. This is why Folex (pH 9-10) is not used on seat belts even though it's effective on other materials. The alkalinity, applied repeatedly over years of cleanings, would gradually strip the FR treatment. It's also why rinse water pH matters. FR-treated polyester webbing has a baseline pH of approximately 5.5-6.5 — the phosphate-phosphonate chemistry is mildly acidic on the substrate. Rinsing with neutral pH 7.0 water pushes the substrate alkaline relative to its baseline. Repeated cleaning with pH-mismatched water shifts the webbing chemistry off baseline over time, eventually affecting the FR treatment. The rinse water for seat belts is targeted at pH 5.5-6.0 to match the FR-treated baseline. Open-bottle distilled water naturally lands in this range — atmospheric CO2 dissolves into the water and forms carbonic acid, dropping fresh-sealed distilled (pH 7.0) down to approximately 5.5-6.0 within 10-30 minutes of the cap coming off. The procedure below verifies this with the YINMIK probe before cleaning and after rinsing.

Why This Goes Beyond AmSafe's Recommendation AmSafe's published cleaning guidance — warm water, mild household soap, hand-wash, no IPA, no machine washing, no soap on metal — is the general operator standard. It's written for flight crews, A&Ps, and line technicians. The guidance is designed to prevent the failures AmSafe sees in the field: hardware corrosion, IPA-damaged fibers, dryer-damaged webbing. AmSafe's recommended cleaners are within the stable FR range (pH 4-8) — they won't actively degrade the phosphate-phosphonate treatment. But mild dish soap at pH 7-8 sits at the alkaline edge of that range,

shifted from the substrate's 5.5-6.5 baseline. There's a difference between stable and matched. The pH-matched rinse adds three things AmSafe's general guidance doesn't:
1. Verified residue removal — the YINMIK probe confirms the cleaner is fully out of the fibers, not assumed from visual inspection.
2. Substrate returned to baseline — not just kept within the stable range, but returned to where the FR chemistry was designed to sit.
3. Compounding benefit over high-frequency cleaning — most operators clean a seat belt twice in its 10-year AmSafe service life. A flight department's regular detailer touches the same belt 20+ times. At that frequency, "slightly off-baseline every clean" accumulates into measurable substrate shift in a way it doesn't for the occasional operator. We're being more careful than AmSafe specifies. Not correcting them. Their guidance is right for what it's written for — the broad operator base. We go further because we touch the same belts repeatedly over years on the same aircraft. Honest caveat: The FR-preservation argument is grounded in how the chemistry behaves (phosphate-phosphonate stability across pH, hydrolysis kinetics outside that range), not in measured outcomes from a controlled study of belts cleaned at different pH protocols over a full service life. It's logical optimization, not validated science. We document the work either way — pH readings get recorded in the CRM with the service. Customer-facing framing: "We follow AmSafe's published guidance for seat belt cleaning, and we go further — pH-matched rinse water and verified post-clean substrate pH protect the fire retardant treatment over the full service life of the belt. Most aviation detailers don't measure pH at all. We do." Seat belt webbing is thin enough that any liquid that lands on it — coffee, soda, soap, water — penetrates the full thickness. That's just the fiber. The procedure below uses the right cleaner for the stain, rinses with pH-matched water, and verifies the substrate is back to baseline before drying. Bridgepoint Coffee Stain Remover (pH 4.0-4.5) is water-based, wool-safe, FR-safe, and dye-safe, which is why it's the right call for coffee specifically.

Setup
1. Bucket of warm water with mild soap dissolved (dishwashing liquid or laundry detergent — small amount, enough to produce minimal suds).
2. Open-bottle distilled water for rinsing — atmospheric CO2 absorption brings it to approximately pH 5.5-6.0, which matches FR-treated webbing baseline. If using a freshly opened jug, leave the cap off for 30 minutes before use, or add 1-2 drops of white vinegar per pint and probe to confirm.
3. YINMIK Digital pH Meter (same probe used in SOP #4) — for baseline check, stain diagnosis, rinse water verification, and post-rinse confirmation.
4. White vinegar in a dropper bottle — for adjusting rinse water pH if needed.
5. Bridgepoint Coffee Stain Remover (pH 4.0-4.5) for coffee.
6. Horsehair brush.
7. Cotton terry washcloths (multiple).
8. Cotton terry hand towels for blotting and laying out.
9. Quick Turn for cleaning buckle and hardware.

10. Large Dreadnaught towel — laid over the cushion so the seat belt isn't sitting on the leather while it's being cleaned.
11. Carpet fan or shop fan for drying.

Procedure Seat belt webbing is thin. Any liquid that lands on it penetrates the full thickness — that's the fiber, not something the procedure can avoid. The right call is to use the cleaner that actually removes the stain, rinse with pH-matched water to bring the substrate back to its baseline, then dry properly. The pH workflow protects the FR treatment over the life of the belt.
1. Pull the seat belt out and lay it across the Dreadnaught towel on the cushion.
2. Verify rinse water pH (5.5-6.0): Probe the open-bottle distilled water with the YINMIK. Atmospheric-equilibrated distilled is usually right in this band. If high (above 6.5), add 1-2 drops of white vinegar per pint and re-probe.
3. Baseline pH read: Place a drop of the pH-matched rinse water on a clean section of the belt webbing. Let it absorb 10 seconds, probe with the YINMIK. Record the reading — that's the baseline for this belt. Most FR-treated polyester webbing reads around pH 5.5-6.5; baselines vary by manufacturer, FR loading, age, and prior cleaning history.
4. Stain pH read: Place a drop on the stain itself, probe, record. This tells you how far the spill drove the substrate and in which direction (acidic spill like coffee or soda drops the reading; alkaline spill raises it).
5. Apply the appropriate cleaner: - Coffee: Bridgepoint Coffee Stain Remover with the horsehair brush. Acidic (pH 4.0-4.5), wool-safe, FR-safe, dye-safe. The acidic chemistry is what breaks the tannin bond — same reason it works on coffee in wool carpet. - General soil, skin oils, food: mild soap solution with the horsehair brush.
6. Light agitation with the horsehair brush. Both sides of the webbing, full length of the stained section. The cleaner penetrates through the belt — that's how it gets the stain out.
7. Rinse with pH-matched water. Damp wrung-out cotton terry washcloths in the open-bottle distilled water (pH 5.5-6.0). Multiple passes with fresh cloths until no cleaner residue remains in the fibers.
8. Post-rinse verification: Place a drop of pH-matched rinse water on the cleaned section, probe with YINMIK. Confirm within ±0.5 of the baseline reading from step 3.
9. Off-baseline? Additional rinse pass with fresh damp cotton terry in pH-matched water, then re-verify. Repeat until within range.
10. Pat dry with cotton terry. Press and lift — do not rub or wring the webbing.
11. Clean the buckle and hardware with Quick Turn on a microfiber towel. Spray Quick Turn onto the towel, wipe the buckle face, latch, slide, and end fittings. Light pressure. Soap and water never touch the metal parts — that's AmSafe's rule.

Drying
1. Lay the belt flat on a fresh dry cotton terry towel on the cushion (over the Dreadnaught).
2. Position a fan to move air across the belt.
3. Let it dry. Thin webbing with airflow dries quickly.

4. Confirm fully dry by touch before retracting. Any moisture against leather will mark it.
5. Retract the belt to its normal stored position. If the cabin dehumidifier is running for a carpet drying job, leverage it — lay the belt across the Dreadnaught and let the dry-air environment speed drying further.

Heavier Stains If soap and water don't lift a stain, Koch Chemie Polstar can be used as a fallback. Polstar is a leather cleaner — using it on webbing is off-label, so:
- Confirm the aircraft's AMM does not prohibit it
- Get verbal customer or operator approval
- Patch test on the underside of the belt
- Rinse with pH-matched water (5.5-6.0) using damp wrung-out cotton terry — multiple passes with fresh cloths until no Polstar residue remains in the fibers
- Verify post-rinse pH with YINMIK probe — confirm within ±0.5 of the belt's baseline before drying
- Document the cleaner used in the CRM

If the Leather Has Already Been Conditioned If conditioning (Section 14) is already complete and a damp belt contacts the seat, slight discoloration of the leather can occur. Conditioning happens last for exactly this reason.

## 13. Suede, Micro Suede, and Alcantara Cleaning

Suede, micro suede, and Alcantara are open-pored materials with no protective clearcoat. They share the same cleaning method as leather because Polstar is essentially a light soap — gentle enough to use safely on both pigmented leather and open-pored surfaces. One product handles the whole seat.

Procedure
1. Spray Koch Chemie Polstar directly onto the suede / micro suede / Alcantara.
2. Scrub with a horsehair brush from multiple angles to lift the soil. Fibers lay in one direction, so brushing from different angles reaches contamination from all sides.
3. Wipe the horsehair brush clean with a microfiber towel between passes.
4. Dry with the MetroVac blower (from SOP #4 carpet cleaning kit). The MetroVac has no temperature setting — it generates only the mild heat from its own motor, which is not enough to scorch fibers. Use it moving, never parked.
5. While drying, continue brushing with the horsehair brush. This is critical — drying suede or Alcantara without brushing leaves it hard and matted. Brushing while drying restores the soft, velvety texture.
6. Continue until completely dry and soft.

Suede / Alcantara on Headliner or Glued Panels If the material is glued to a surface (headliner, panel inserts, side bolsters), be cautious with the MetroVac blower. The MetroVac doesn't get hot enough to melt adhesive on its own — but combined with agitation from brushing and the mild ambient heat from the motor over time, prolonged exposure in one spot can still soften some adhesives. Keep the nozzle moving constantly, hold it further from the surface, and never linger in one spot.

## 14. Leather Conditioning (Last Step)

Conditioning is the final step on every seat — done only after the leather, stitching, embroidery, and seat belts are clean and dry.

Procedure
1. Open the tin of Colourlock Elephant Leather Preserver. Load the microfiber applicator pad with a small amount of the paste-form preserver.
2. Gently rub the preserver into the leather, working in even sections across the seat. Cover every leather surface — armrests, cushion, seat back, headrest, side panels.
3. Conditioning over stitching is okay. The preserver does not harm stitching.
4. Conditioning over embroidery is NOT okay. Avoid the embroidered area — the preserver can affect the embroidery's appearance.
5. After the entire seat is conditioned, buff the leather with a clean multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber towel. Buffing serves two purposes: - Removes excess preserver (which would otherwise attract dirt) - Restores the smooth, luxurious feel of the leather
6. Inspect under headlamp. Reapply preserver to any panel that absorbed too much, then re-buff. Move to the next seat.

## 15. Quality Control Walkaround

Walk the cabin in the same direction as the pre-service.
- Every seat cleaned, dried, and conditioned
- No visible cleaner residue, streaks, or dampness on any leather
- Stitching cleaned and fully dry
- Embroidery cleaned and undamaged
- Seat belts cleaned and dressed
- Suede, micro suede, or Alcantara soft to the touch, no matting

## 16. Critical Don'ts

- Don't start any service before the pre-service CRM photo upload and walkaround.
- Don't leave the aircraft before the post-service CRM upload is complete.
- Don't do leather work the same day you've polished brightwork. Polish residue stays on clothing and tools and can permanently stain leather, fabric, headliners, and other interior surfaces. Either swap detailers or fully decontaminate.
- Don't patch-test on a high-traffic seat and assume the result applies to inconspicuous areas, or vice versa. Clearcoat wear varies dramatically by seat.
- Don't clean an entire seat in one go before wiping. Panel by panel — wipe the foam and cleaner up before it dries. Dried cleaner lets the lifted dirt re-adhere to the leather.
- Don't oversaturate the Scrub Ninja or any towel. Damp is correct. Dripping is wrong.
- Don't use Colourlock Strong Leather Cleaner as a default. Polstar is the default; Strong is reserved for stains Polstar can't lift.
- Don't continue cleaning if color transfers onto the towel — stop and notify the customer. The leather's clearcoat or dye is failing.
- Don't scrub stitching aggressively. The brush + light pressure is enough.
- Don't let the Tornador nozzle contact leather. The spinning metal nozzle inside the cone will damage it. Hold ~1" (2.5 cm) off stitching, ~2" (5 cm) off embroidery.
- Don't operate the Tornador without respirator and safety glasses. Aircraft doors must always be open with a fan running.
- Don't operate the Tornador without a fan running and aircraft doors open.
- Don't carry seat belt water into freshly conditioned leather. Belts dry fully before they retract back onto a conditioned seat.
- Don't clean seat belt webbing with any cleaner not approved by the belt manufacturer or the aircraft's maintenance manual without written customer/operator approval. Chemical contamination of webbing makes the belt unairworthy.

- Don't machine-wash a seat belt. Ever. Per AmSafe and other OEMs, machine washing changes the webbing's performance characteristics.
- Don't soak seat belt hardware in soap and water. Hardware is wiped with Quick Turn on a microfiber towel.
- Don't clean the buckle, latch, slide, or end fittings with soap and water. AmSafe explicitly prohibits soap or water on metal parts. Use Quick Turn on a microfiber towel for hardware.
- Don't rinse seat belt webbing with pH 7.0 (neutral) water. FR-treated webbing baseline is pH 5.5-6.5 — neutral water is alkaline relative to baseline and shifts the substrate over time, degrading the FR treatment. Use open-bottle distilled water at pH 5.5-6.0, verified with YINMIK.
- Don't use Folex on seat belt webbing. Folex is pH 9-10 — too alkaline for FR-treated polyester. Repeated use degrades the phosphate-phosphonate FR chemistry that allows the belt to meet FAR 25.853.
- Don't skip the post-rinse pH verification. The baseline check and post-rinse confirm are what protect the FR treatment over years of cleaning.
- Don't skip checking the aircraft's maintenance manual or operator-approved cleaner list before cleaning a seat belt.
- Don't condition over embroidery.
- Don't park the MetroVac blower on suede, micro suede, or Alcantara that's glued to a panel. The MetroVac doesn't get hot enough on its own to melt adhesive, but combined with brushing agitation and prolonged exposure in one spot, some adhesives can soften. Keep the nozzle moving.
- Don't linger the MetroVac in one spot on any open-pored material (suede, micro suede, Alcantara). Move the nozzle continuously while brushing.
- Don't skip the pre-vacuum step. Wet cleaning over crumbs grinds them into the leather.
