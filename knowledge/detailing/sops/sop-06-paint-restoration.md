## 1. Purpose

Aircraft paint loses gloss and develops defects over time. Sun exposure, fuel and oil residue, airflow at altitude, abrasive cleaning, and environmental contamination all combine to break down the top of the clearcoat (or in older aircraft, the single-stage pigmented paint itself). The result: oxidation, water spots, micro-marring, swirls, and dullness.

Paint restoration removes the damaged surface layer by layer until the paint underneath is uniform and clear. It is the service that follows paint decontamination (SOP #5) and precedes any sealant, wax, or ceramic coating. Decon makes the paint chemically clean; restoration makes it optically clean.

## 2. Paint Types and Their Implications

Aircraft typically have one of two paint systems:

- Single-stage — the standard across most aircraft, including modern aircraft. Pigment and resin in a single layer with no clearcoat. Single-stage is preferred in aviation because it's lighter than a base coat + clear coat system. Weight matters on an aircraft in a way it doesn't on a car, so what the automotive world treats as "older" technology is actually the modern aviation standard.
- Clearcoat (base coat / clear coat) — less common in aviation; found on some aircraft where the operator or builder chose it deliberately. A pigmented base coat with a clear protective layer on top. Polishing works the clearcoat; the pigment below is not directly touched.

Identifying which you have:

- Color transfer test: Apply polish to a small area, work it briefly, wipe with a microfiber. If the towel picks up paint color, it's single-stage. If the towel is clean, it's clearcoat.
- Owner / pilot confirmation: Ask. Most owners know.
- Visual cues: Clearcoat has a depth and "wet" look even when dull. Single-stage looks chalky when oxidized, with no depth — but a well-maintained single-stage paint job can look outstanding and is what's on most aircraft you'll work on.

The pad, product, and tool choice depends on the paint type. They are not interchangeable.

Pad note: the SPTA wool pad is DA-specific — designed for the orbital motion of a dual-action polisher. The wool pad used on the rotary is a different pad designed for direct rotation. They are not interchangeable. Using a DA-specific pad on a rotary, or a rotary-specific pad on a DA, gives poor cut and uneven finish.

The single-stage one-step is the SPTA wool pad + Menzerna 400 on the Milwaukee DA. This is the default starting point. The DA's orbital motion delivers an even cut. Step up to the rotary with a rotary wool pad and Menzerna 400 only when oxidation doesn't lift on the DA alone.

Clearcoat needs two pads when defects are present — a rotary wool pad with ACA 500 on the rotary for cutting, followed by a yellow foam pad with Fly Shiny Polish Pro on the Milwaukee DA for finishing. The DA + foam removes the holograms and micro-marring the rotary + wool leaves behind.

## 4. The Step-Back Principle

Always start with the least aggressive combination that can fix the defect and step up only if needed.

For each panel:

1. Try the lightest combination first (polish + foam pad on DA).
2. Inspect under headlamp and isopropyl alcohol wipe (see Section 8).
3. If the defect remains, step up to a more aggressive combination.
4. Once the defect is gone, step back down for the finish pass.

How to Increase Aggressiveness There are three controls. In order of preference:

1. Step up the pad or compound — the cleanest way to add cut. Move from foam to wool, or from polish to compound.
2. Slow down your tempo / arm speed — running the tool more slowly across the panel extends contact time and lets the abrasives do more work. This is the preferred way to add cut on a single pass.
3. More pressure — last resort, and minimal at that. Pressure is the most likely cause of burned paint and the most likely to leave inconsistent results. Pressing harder reduces the DA's orbital motion (the tool stops orbiting and just spins), increases heat, and loads the pad unevenly.

The rule: slow the arm down before you press down. If slowing your tempo and using the right pad/ compound combination isn't getting the result, step up the pad or compound — don't push harder.

Single-stage step sequence:

1. One-step / standard finish: SPTA wool pad + Menzerna 400 on the Milwaukee DA. This is the default — the DA's orbital motion gives an even cut without holograms.
2. If oxidation doesn't fully lift on the DA: slow your arm speed and run another pass on the DA. Often this is enough.
3. If still not lifting: step up to the rotary with a rotary wool pad and Menzerna 400. Swap pads when changing tools — the SPTA pad stays on the DA and the rotary wool pad stays on the rotary.

Clearcoat step sequence:

1. Light defects: Yellow foam pad + Fly Shiny Polish Pro on Milwaukee DA. Single pass for finish.
2. Medium / heavy oxidation, water spots, deeper defects: Wool pad + ACA 500 on rotary first, then step down to yellow foam pad + Polish Pro on the DA to remove the rotary's holograms and refine the finish.

The principle: single-stage one-step is the DA + wool + Menzerna 400. Step up to the rotary with the same pad and product if the DA alone doesn't lift oxidation. For clearcoat, the rotary + wool + ACA 500 is the cut, and the DA + yellow foam + Polish Pro is the finish. Never leave a clearcoat aircraft with rotary holograms — always finish with the DA step.

## 5. Tools & Equipment

Polishing tools

- Milwaukee dual-action battery-powered polisher — default tool for single-stage one-step (wool pad + Menzerna 400) and for clearcoat finishing (yellow foam + Polish Pro). Same Milwaukee unit used in SOP
#4 (carpet agitation) and SOP #5 (clay bar pad).
- Rotary buffer — step up from the DA when single-stage oxidation doesn't lift with the DA alone, and primary cutting tool for heavy oxidation on clearcoat with ACA 500.
- Pads — Pads are tool-specific and not interchangeable:

- SPTA wool pad — Milwaukee DA only. Designed for orbital motion. Used for single-stage one-step with Menzerna 400.
- Rotary wool pad — rotary only. Designed for direct rotation. Used for single-stage heavy oxidation (Menzerna 400) and clearcoat heavy cutting (ACA 500).
- Yellow foam pad — Milwaukee DA, clearcoat finishing
- Extra pads on hand — change pads before they load up with spent product and removed paint material. A loaded pad cuts unevenly.
- Rag Company Tornador-style pad-cleaning tool — used with straight compressed air only, no solution tank. Blows spent product and removed paint material out of the pad's fibers between sections. Required for clean cuts.
- Pad-raking brush — used on wool pads to separate caked fibers. Wool fibers mat together as they pick up compound; raking pulls them apart so each fiber cuts independently. Used in conjunction with compressed-air cleaning.
- Air compressor or air source for the pad cleaner.

Products

- Menzerna 400 — single-stage polish/compound
- ACA 500 — clearcoat compound (most aggressive)
- Fly Shiny Polish Pro — clearcoat finishing polish

Inspection

- Isopropyl alcohol mixed 1:1 with water in a spray bottle — wipes off polish residue so the bare paint can be inspected. Etched-in water spots and deep defects only become visible after the alcohol wipe — polish oils mask them temporarily.
- Headlamp — inspection under raking light.
- Autofiber Motherfluffer — primary towel for wiping polish and compound off paint. The reasons it's the standard:
- Dense fibers — more surface area picks up more product per pass
- Soft, plush construction — disperses hand pressure across the towel face so finger-pressure points don't dig into the paint
- Sewn-inside edges — no exposed stitching to scratch the paint
- Removes polish and compound effortlessly and leaves a clean surface for alcohol-wipe inspection
- Multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber towels — general utility (priming pads, between-task cleanup) and the dedicated towel for the alcohol-mix wipe in Section 10. The Motherfluffer stays dry for polish/compound wipe-off only; the multi-purpose towel handles anything damp.

### Other

- Ladder tall enough for the aircraft
- Creeper for low panels
- Painter's tape, 2" (5 cm) — masking decals, registration markings, edges adjacent to brightwork or boots (full mask is appropriate here — polish residue on these is harder to remove than decon residue)

- Mobile device with Shiny Jets CRM access

Pre-staging

- All pads clean. No cross-contamination between wool and foam pads.
- All tools and the detailer's clothing free of brightwork polish residue and prior-job contamination.

## 6. PPE

PPE must meet recognized international occupational safety standards.

- Eye protection: Safety glasses meeting ANSI Z87.1 or EN 166. Polish spits off both rotary and DA pads, especially at higher RPM.
- Hand protection: Nitrile gloves meeting EN ISO 374-1, minimum 6 mil. Protects skin from compound and keeps oils/contamination off the freshly polished paint.
- Respirator (optional): Compound dust from heavy cutting on a dry pad can become airborne. A respirator is recommended for extended rotary work in enclosed spaces.
- Foot protection: Closed-toe non-marking work shoes meeting EN ISO 20345 or ASTM F2413.
- Hearing protection: EN 352-1 or ANSI S3.19 rated. Rotary buffers run for long periods and the noise adds up; if working near an APU or adjacent running aircraft, hearing protection is required.
- Clothing: Long pants and long sleeves recommended. Polish residue and removed paint can stain.

## 8. Aircraft Preparation

1. Aircraft must already be decontaminated (SOP #5). Polishing over bonded contamination drives that contamination across the paint and produces deep scratches.

2. Mask: - Pitot tubes and static ports - Decals, registration markings, placards - Lenses (nav lights, landing lights, beacons) - Door seals and window seals - Borders adjacent to brightwork and de-ice boots — polishing compound is harder to remove from those surfaces than decon residue. Full mask of the boot/ brightwork edge with 2" (5 cm) tape is appropriate here. - Composite panels with sensitive finishes - Any fresh paint repairs (under 30 days)

3. Position the ladder and creeper for the panels you'll be working.

4. Stage pads and products by tool: - Rotary station: rotary wool pads, Menzerna 400 (single-stage) or ACA 500 (clearcoat) - DA station: SPTA wool pads (for single-stage one-step) and yellow foam pads (for clearcoat finishing), Menzerna 400 and Fly Shiny Polish Pro - Keep pads physically separated by tool. SPTA pads stay on the DA side, rotary wool pads stay on the rotary side. Pads are tool-specific and cross-contamination between cutting compound and finishing polish causes finishing problems.

5. Seat each pad firmly onto the backing plate. Press the pad flat against the hook-and-loop and apply firm pressure across its full diameter. A pad that's not fully seated will flex unevenly during use, which

strips the hook-and-loop fastener on the back of the pad and shortens the pad's service life. Properly seated, the pad and backing plate act as one piece.

6. Pre-prime the pads lightly with product per the product manufacturer's instructions. A bone-dry pad on first contact can scratch.

7. Have Autofiber Motherfluffers and multi-purpose microfiber towels and the isopropyl alcohol spray bottle staged within reach.

8. Confirm hands, gloves, and clothing are free of brightwork polish residue and prior-job contamination.

## 9. Polishing Procedure

Work the top surfaces first — top of the wings, fuselage top, horizontal stabilizer, top of nacelles. Then verticals. Then any underside surfaces being serviced this interval.

Work section by section — typically 2'×2' (about 60×60 cm) or smaller. Larger sections lose polish to evaporation before you can work it. The pad must stay on flat panel surfaces — same rivet-line and panel-transition rules as decon (SOP #5 Section 8).

Per-Section Procedure
1. Apply the product: - DA polisher: 3–5 pea-sized drops directly onto the pad face. Spread across the section with the pad off, then engage the tool. - Rotary with wool pad (compound): apply the compound to the paint, not to the pad. Compound applied directly to a wool pad cakes the fibers together at the base, which produces a pad that cuts deeper holograms — caked fibers act as larger, harder points than individual fibers do. Applying to the paint keeps the fibers separated and lets fresh product reach the panel each pass. Rake the pad regularly to separate the fibers — see Pad Changes and Cleaning (later in this section).
2. Start at low speed (rotary: ~600 rpm; DA: low setting). This spreads the product across the section without slinging.
3. Increase to working speed (rotary: 900–1200 rpm; DA: one speed below top speed). Make slow, overlapping passes — about 50% overlap, working in cross-hatch direction (horizontal then vertical). The cross-hatch isn't about streaks — it's about getting an even cut or polish across the full section, since a single-direction pass tends to cut more in the direction of travel than perpendicular to it.
4. Arm tempo: approximately 1 inch (2.5 cm) per second. This is the standard. Slower than this and the abrasives have nothing left to do; faster and the cut becomes inconsistent. To add cut, slow down further before adding pressure.
5. Light pressure on both the rotary and the DA. Let the tool's weight do the work. Pressing harder is the most common cause of burned paint and inconsistent results; on the DA, pressure also reduces orbital motion.

6. Work each section for up to ~45 seconds. Compounds and polishes lose their effectiveness past that point — the abrasives have fully broken down. If the section needs more cut, wipe it off, reapply fresh product, and run another ~45-second pass. Repeat until the desired result is achieved.
7. Wipe the section with a clean Autofiber Motherfluffer. The Motherfluffer's dense fibers and sewn-inside edges remove polish/compound cleanly without scratching.
8. Inspect — see Section 10.

Per-Tool Speeds and Cautions
- Rotary: ~600 rpm to start, 900–1200 rpm for working speed on aircraft paint. The rotary generates heat fast. Move the pad continuously — stopping on a single spot for even a second can burn the paint, especially on hot panels or thin clearcoats.
- Milwaukee DA: low for spread, one speed below top speed for working. Full top speed is reserved for the final finishing pass on finished panels only. The DA is gentler than a rotary but can still heat up paint with excessive pressure.

Edges, Rivets, and Raised Features — Burn Risk Edges and rivets burn fast with any tool, especially the rotary. Heat concentrates on raised features because there's less paint mass to dissipate it. Pad pressure also tends to focus on edges as the pad rolls slightly off a flat surface.

For any tool — rotary or DA:

- Keep the pad away from edges of panels, leading edges, trailing edges, and any sharp transition. Hand-polish those areas with a microfiber applicator if they need attention.
- Never run the pad directly over a rivet line. A rotary will burn the rivet itself or the paint at its base; a DA can mar the surrounding clearcoat. Stay off rivet rows entirely with the powered tool.
- The rotary is the worst offender. A rotary pad rolling over a rivet or edge can burn through the paint to bare metal in less than a second. Plan your panel work so the rotary stays in the middle of flat sections.
- Move continuously. Even on safe flat panel, never let the tool dwell in one place.

Pad Changes and Cleaning
- Clean the pad with compressed air after every section (wool) or every 2–3 sections (foam). Use the Rag Company Tornador-style pad-cleaning tool with no solution tank — straight air only. Compressed air blows spent product and removed paint material out of the fibers without wetting the pad. Skipping this is the fastest way to ruin a finish: a loaded pad doesn't cut evenly and drags removed material back across the panel as fresh micro-marring.
- Wool pads (both SPTA on the DA and rotary wool): clean every section — wool loads fast.
- Yellow foam pad on the DA: clean every 2–3 sections — foam holds less product so it stays clean longer.

- Rake wool pads with a pad-raking brush to separate the fibers. Wool fibers cake together as they pick up compound; raking pulls them apart so each fiber cuts independently. A raked pad cuts evenly; a caked pad cuts deeper holograms.
- Swap to a fresh pad when compressed air and raking no longer bring the pad back to clean, or when the pad becomes saturated and doesn't cut.

## 10. Inspection — The Alcohol Wipe

Polish oils mask defects temporarily. A panel can look perfect immediately after polishing and reveal water spots, holograms, or micro-marring once the oils evaporate. The isopropyl alcohol wipe forces the truth.

For each section:

1. After wiping the polish/compound off with the Motherfluffer, spray a light mist of isopropyl alcohol mixed 1:1 with water onto the panel.
2. Wipe with a clean multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber towel — not the Motherfluffer. The Motherfluffer is reserved for polish/compound wipe-off and must stay dry; alcohol mix on the Motherfluffer compromises its fiber loft and reduces its effectiveness on subsequent wipe-offs. The multi-purpose towel handles the alcohol mix and the silicone residue it picks up.
3. Inspect under headlamp at a low angle (raking light) and from multiple angles.

What you're looking for:

- Etched water spots — sunken into the paint, visible after alcohol wipe even though they may have looked gone after polishing. These often require more aggressive cutting or, if deep enough, cannot be fully removed.
- Holograms — circular swirl patterns left by the rotary. Always present after rotary work on clearcoat. Removed by the DA + foam + Polish Pro finishing step.
- Micro-marring — fine scratches in a uniform pattern, typically from a too-aggressive cut or a loaded pad.
- Remaining oxidation — dullness, haze, color non-uniformity.

If defects remain after alcohol wipe:

- Step up the cut (more aggressive pad/product) and re-polish that section.
- Then step back down to the finishing combination for the final pass.

If the alcohol wipe reads clean: the section is done.

## 11. Finishing Considerations

Working Adjacent to Brightwork and De-Ice Boots If those surfaces are not being serviced this interval and have been masked off, the rotary or DA can be run close to the mask. Watch the mask line — polish that gets under the tape onto brightwork or boots is harder to remove than decon residue and stays there until those surfaces are serviced.

If a section is too close to brightwork or boots for the mask alone, switch to hand-polishing with a microfiber applicator. Slower, but it gives full control.

Working Environment and Heat All polishing work must be done in shade or inside a hangar. Never polish in direct sunlight. Direct sun on paint causes two problems:

- Overheated panels — sun-heated paint reaches temperatures where polishing can cause embrittlement of the clearcoat or single-stage layer, weakening the paint long-term.
- Compound and polish flash off too fast in the heat — the abrasives dry on the surface before they finish working, and the cut becomes inconsistent.

In shade or in a hangar, ambient temperature is manageable. The risk that remains is isolated heat: heat generated locally by the spinning pad. Isolated heat is the real polishing concern, not panel temperature alone.

Isolated heat causes:

- Burned paint — even a moving pad can generate enough localized heat on a single spot to burn through clearcoat in seconds, especially on edges, rivets, and raised features (less paint mass to dissipate the heat into).
- Inconsistent cut — heat changes how the compound breaks down. A pad that lingers in one spot creates an inconsistent cut zone.

Manage isolated heat:

- Keep the pad moving at all times. Continuous motion spreads the heat across the section.
- Watch for edges, rivets, and raised features where heat concentrates. See "Edges, Rivets, and Raised Features — Burn Risk" above.
- Touch the panel with the back of your hand between sections. If a section feels warm to the touch, give it a minute to cool before continuing. A cool panel handles polish better than a warm one.

Compound vs Polish The matrix above (Section 3) tells you when to use compound (ACA 500 on clearcoat; Menzerna 400 on single-stage) vs polish (Fly Shiny Polish Pro on clearcoat). The general rule: compound cuts, polish finishes. Never finish a clearcoat aircraft with only the compound step — always finish with the DA + foam + Polish Pro to get the optical clarity the paint is capable of.

## 12. Quality Control

Walk the aircraft and confirm:

- All masking and covers removed
- No polish residue on decals, brightwork mask edges, lenses, rivet lines, or seams
- Alcohol wipe inspection passes on all polished panels — no holograms, no significant remaining defects, no etched water spots that weren't disclosed to the customer
- Pre-existing defects (chips, deep scratches, clearcoat failure) documented in CRM are still documented post-restoration
- No new damage introduced — burned clearcoat, holograms left from skipped finishing step, edges polished through to base coat
- Pitot tubes and static ports uncovered and confirmed clear
- All tools, ladders, creepers, hoses, and pads removed from around the aircraft

Critical Don'ts

- Don't polish paint that hasn't been decontaminated. Bonded contamination becomes scratches under the pad.
- Don't mix paint types — never use single-stage products (Menzerna 400) on clearcoat as the finishing step, or clearcoat products on single-stage as the cutting step. Use the matrix in Section 3.
- Don't finish a clearcoat aircraft on the rotary. Rotary work leaves holograms; the DA + foam + Polish Pro finishing step is what removes them.

- Don't put a DA-specific pad on the rotary or a rotary-specific pad on the DA. The SPTA wool pad is designed for orbital motion and the rotary wool pad is designed for direct rotation. Mixing them gives poor cut and uneven finish.
- Don't mount a pad without seating it firmly onto the backing plate. A partially seated pad flexes during use and strips the hook-and-loop on its back, which kills the pad's service life early.
- Don't skip the alcohol wipe. Polish oils mask defects; the alcohol wipe is the only honest inspection.
- Don't wipe polish or compound off with a general-utility microfiber, edged towel, or any towel with exposed stitching. The Autofiber Motherfluffer is the standard — its sewn-inside edges and dense fiber face prevent scratching, and its plush construction disperses hand pressure across the towel face. The wrong towel leaves swirls that the alcohol wipe will reveal.
- Don't skip pad cleaning between sections. Wool pads get cleaned every section; foam pads every 2–3 sections. Skip the cleaning and the loaded pad drags removed material back across the panel as fresh marring.
- Don't put solution in the Tornador-style pad cleaner. Straight air only. Adding water or any solution wets the pad and ruins the cleaning effect.
- Don't work a single section longer than ~45 seconds. Compounds and polishes lose their effectiveness past that point — wipe off, reapply fresh product, and start a new pass if more cut is needed.
- Don't rush the arm tempo. The standard is ~1 inch (2.5 cm) per second — faster than that and the cut becomes inconsistent.
- Don't run the rotary or DA over rivets, panel edges, or leading/trailing edges. Heat concentrates on raised features and burn-through happens fast — the rotary worst of all. Hand-polish those areas if they need attention.
- Don't polish in direct sunlight. All polishing must be done in shade or inside a hangar. Direct sun on paint overheats panels (risking embrittlement) and flashes off compound and polish before they finish working.
- Don't let the pad dwell on one spot. Isolated heat from the spinning pad — not ambient temperature — is the cause of burned paint. Keep the pad moving continuously.
- Don't press hard to get more cut. Slow your arm speed instead. Pressure is the most common cause of burned paint and inconsistent results. Pressing on the DA also reduces orbital motion — the tool stops orbiting and just spins.
- Don't promise a customer that all etched water spots will come out. Note them in CRM during the walkaround and set expectations.
- Don't leave restored paint exposed overnight without protection. Move to the customer's chosen sealant, wax, or ceramic coating the same day, or cover the aircraft until the protection step can be done.
