# SOP #11 — Brightwork Polishing

**Service:** Polishing of Polished Aluminum Brightwork (Leading Edges, Engine Inlets, Cuffs, Trim)  
**Author:** Shiny Jets  
**Source PDF:** `SOP-11-Brightwork-Polishing.pdf`

_Shop standard operating procedure for Detailing AI. Prefer this over generic advice when diagnosing or quoting this service._

## 1. Purpose

Brightwork is polished bare aluminum — leading edges, engine inlets, nacelle lips, polished fairings, trim. Unlike painted surfaces it has no clearcoat protecting it; the polished aluminum surface itself is what reflects light. Over time it oxidizes, develops water spotting, scratches, and pitting, and dulls. The Shiny Jets brightwork system uses four tools arranged on an aggressiveness ladder, each paired with specific pads and metal polishes. The non-negotiable rule: always start at the least aggressive step and step up only when the level isn't enough. Cutting more aggressively than needed wastes time — every level you step up the aggressiveness adds refinement steps on the way back down.

## 2. The Aggressiveness Ladder — Tools

From least to most aggressive:

Level     Tool                                      Cut

1         Ryobi 10" battery-powered                 Lightest — finish refinement and light cut random orbital polisher

2         Dual-action (DA) polisher with 5"         Medium — refinement and moderate cut backing plate

3         Pneumatic drum buffer                     Heavy cut with continuous cooling (the drum's rotation lifts the pad off the surface intermittently)

4         Milwaukee rotary buffer (battery-         Heaviest — used when heat generation is needed for the powered)                                  deepest pitting and oxidation

## 3. The Polishing Method

The method has two phases:
1. Step up the aggressiveness from least aggressive until the defect is cut out.

2. Step back down the aggressiveness to refine the finish. How far you have to step back down depends on the alloy:
- Soft aluminum — even after heavy cutting, you can often jump straight to the 10" Ryobi random orbital to finish. The soft alloy refines easily.
- Hard aluminum — you can't skip levels on the way back down. After rotary cutting you may need to step down to the DA with microfiber pad (sometimes finishing there), and may still need to follow with the 10" Ryobi random orbital to complete the job. If you're unsure of the alloy, treat it as hard and step down through each level.

## 6. PPE

- Eye protection: Safety glasses meeting ANSI Z87.1 or EN 166. Metal polish flings off spinning pads.
- Hand protection: Nitrile gloves meeting EN ISO 374-1, minimum 6 mil. Metal polish stains skin black and is hard to remove.
- Foot protection: Closed-toe non-marking work shoes meeting EN ISO 20345 or ASTM F2413.
- Hearing protection: EN 352-1 or ANSI S3.19 rated for sustained drum buffer or rotary use, and when air compressors are running.
- Respiratory: Particulate mask appropriate for sustained brightwork polishing — metal polish aerosol is unpleasant to breathe.
- Clothing: Dedicated brightwork clothing or coveralls. Metal polish residue on clothing transfers to anything you touch later — especially aircraft interiors. Change before going into the cabin.

## 8. Aircraft Preparation

1. Tape all paint adjacent to the brightwork with 2" (5 cm) blue painter's tape. The tape line is critical: - No tape on top of the brightwork — tape on brightwork will lift in spots when the polisher passes and leave an uneven cutline. - No paint left exposed next to the brightwork. The tape covers the paint right up to the brightwork edge.
2. Wipe the panels and weeping edges adjacent to the brightwork with isopropyl alcohol before taping. Alcohol cleans residue off the paint so the tape adheres firmly. This makes a major difference on Hawker 800 series and is a good practice on any aircraft — clean surface = tape stays put = no polish wicking under.
3. Cover all lenses and windows in the polishing area — nav lights, landing lights, beacons, cabin windows. Tape and cover with plastic where needed. Metal polish on a lens or window is hard to remove cleanly.
4. Cover any surface where polish dust or fling can land — metal polish dust travels further than you'd expect off the spinning pads, and it stains paint and contaminates other surfaces. Use plastic sheeting, red contractor's paper, or painter's paper, secured with tape. Whatever's available — the point is full coverage of every surface the polish dust could reach.
5. Stage products by tool, kept physically separated. Each tool's products in their own zone so cross-contamination doesn't happen.
6. Stage clean cotton terry towels in quantity — metal polish wipe-off loads towels fast.
7. Plush microfiber towel kept in a bag with whitening powder — for final residue removal. Metal polish leaves a stubborn residue that cotton terry alone won't lift; the plush microfiber pre-loaded with whitening powder pulls the residue off the polished aluminum cleanly.
8. Aircraft in shade or in a hangar. Same rule as paint restoration. Direct sun flashes off polish too fast and adds unnecessary heat to surfaces.
9. Pre-prime the pads lightly per the product instructions.

## 9. Step-Up — Find the Right Level

Start at the least aggressive level (Ryobi 10") with a light product. Polish a small test area. Wipe with cotton terry and inspect — judge by what's coming out of the surface, not by time elapsed.

Decision logic
- Defect coming out → continue at this level across the section. This is your cutting level.
- Defect not coming out → step up one level on the ladder and repeat the test. Step up logic: - Ryobi 10" with cotton velour bonnet → not enough? → Step up to DA with microfiber pad and the same or stepped-up product. - DA not enough? → Step up to drum buffer, starting with

combo pad. - Combo pad on drum not enough? → Step up to wool pad on the drum with top-cut products. - Wool pad on drum not enough? → Step up to rotary with wool pad (heaviest cut, heat generation). Don't step up further than needed. The right level is the lowest one that cuts the defect in a reasonable time.

Working procedure at any level
1. Apply the chosen product to the pad (or to the surface per the product's instructions).
2. Spreading the polish: - Drum buffer: spread with the tool off, then engage at low speed. The drum's heavy weight and direct drive will sling product if engaged hot. - All other tools (Ryobi, DA, rotary): engage and go — no off-spread step needed.
3. Pad-caking note: when using a heavy NuVite product (F9, F7, A, C) or Fly Shiny Top Cut, add Fly Shiny Maverick to the mix. Maverick acts as a lubricant that reduces caking of metal polish into the pad fibers — keeping the pad cutting cleanly instead of dragging caked product.
4. Light pressure at all tools and all levels. Pressure causes uneven cut and adds unnecessary heat.

## 5. Horizontal passes overlapping 50%. No cross-hatching. On wings, leading edges, and long

brightwork sections, work horizontal passes in line with the chord of the surface, each pass overlapping the previous by 50%.
6. Arm tempo about 1 inch (2.5 cm) per second.
7. No fixed time per pass — judge by eye. Some polishes (heavier ones like F9, F7, Top Cut) work best when fully polished off the surface. Others (light polishes like Reflection, Maverick) work better when you leave a thin film and wipe off. With experience you develop an eye for seeing through the polish to read whether the defect is out. Stop polishing when the defects are visibly gone, not at an arbitrary time.
8. Wipe with cotton terry between passes. Confirm the defect status.

## 10. Step-Down — Refine to a Mirror

After cutting out the defect at the appropriate level, step back down the aggressiveness to refine the finish. The step-down depends on the alloy:

Soft Aluminum Soft alloys refine quickly. Even after the rotary, you can often jump straight to the Ryobi 10" random orbital with cotton velour bonnet and a refinement product (Fly Shiny Reflection, NuVite S, Fly Shiny Maverick) to finish. Watch the finish under good light as you refine — if jumping straight to the Ryobi leaves swirls or holograms, step back up to the DA with microfiber pad first, then come back to the Ryobi.

Hard Aluminum Hard alloys do not allow skipping levels on the way back down. After heavy cutting:
1. Step down to the DA with microfiber pad — refine with a moderate product. Sometimes the job finishes at this level (the DA + microfiber + refinement product can produce a mirror finish on its own).
2. If swirls or holograms remain after the DA, follow with the Ryobi 10" random orbital and cotton velour bonnet for the final refinement.

If You're Unsure of the Alloy Treat it as hard. Step down through each level — that's the safe default. Time spent at the DA on a soft alloy isn't wasted; time spent skipping to the Ryobi on a hard alloy creates rework.

Flash-and-Polish Technique (Reflection, Maverick, Light Polishes) For light polishes — Reflection, Maverick — an alternate application method that works well for the refinement passes:
1. Apply a thin coat of polish by hand with a microfiber towel. Spread evenly across the section.
2. Let it flash — the polish dries / hazes slightly. Usually 30-60 seconds depending on temperature and product.
3. Polish off with the Ryobi 10" random orbital and cotton velour bonnet. Light pressure, machine does the work. Combined technique for a refined finish in one pass:
- Small amount of Striker + 2-3x as much Reflection or Maverick — apply by hand with a microfiber towel, let flash, polish off with the Ryobi 10" RO.
- The Striker adds a touch of cut; the Reflection or Maverick brings the gloss up. Combined and applied-flashed-polished off in one pass produces a clean refinement that's hard to match with machine-only application.

## 11. Heat Management

Most levels of brightwork polishing don't generate heat as a major concern. Two exceptions:
- Pneumatic drum buffer — designed to lift the pad off the surface intermittently, which keeps heat in check. Heat is rarely a problem here.
- Rotary buffer — direct rotation, continuous contact, generates real heat on the aluminum. This is intentional for the deepest defects, but it requires monitoring.

Temperature Rule Keep aluminum surface temperature within 20°F (11°C) of ambient. At 75°F ambient, that's a 95°F surface ceiling. At 100°F ambient day, 120°F ceiling. Why 20°F over ambient:
- Localized heat = thermal stress. Aluminum aircraft skin is thin and conducts heat away quickly when ambient takes the load. Driving a single spot 30, 40, 50°F above ambient creates a thermal gradient

that stresses the immediate area against the cooler surrounding metal. Not enough to cause immediate damage, but unnecessary and avoidable.
- Adjacent paint. Aircraft paint typically begins softening or blistering around 150-200°F sustained. Keeping the polishing temperature well below this protects paint adjacent to the brightwork — the tape line only protects against splatter, not radiant or conducted heat.
- Adjacent sealants. Same range as paint. PRC and similar aerospace sealants degrade under sustained elevated temperature.
- Bonded joints. Some brightwork sits adjacent to or atop bonded skin assemblies. Heat in the brightwork area conducts to the bond layer.
- Metallurgical safety margin. 2024-T3 (most common aircraft skin) overaging starts around 250°F sustained. 20°F over ambient keeps us nowhere near this, but more importantly keeps us nowhere near any temperature that affects the alloy's properties.
- Easy to verify. A laser thermometer or thermal camera reads ambient and surface in seconds. The back-of-hand check is the field shortcut: comfortable to hold = within range, uncomfortably warm = stop.

When Running the Rotary
- Thermal camera or laser thermometer on the surface — read before, during, and after each pass.
- Move continuously. The rotary on a single spot for even a moment can damage the surface.
- Stop the rotary if the surface exceeds 20°F over ambient — wait for it to cool, then resume.
- Stop the rotary if the aluminum is too hot to touch with the back of the hand. This is the field check that matches the thermal reading.

## 12. Pad Cleaning

- Wool pads (drum or rotary): every section. Wool loads fast with cut aluminum and metal polish.
- Combo and cotton pads (drum): every section.
- Microfiber pads (DA): every section.
- Cotton velour bonnets (Ryobi): swap to a fresh bonnet every section — bonnets are rotated out, not deep-cleaned. Rule: clean (or swap) every pad after every section, regardless of pad type. Loaded pads cut unevenly and drag caked product across the next section. The minute it takes to clean or swap is worth it. - Rag Company Tornador-style cleaner, straight air only — no solution tank. - Clean pads outside the immediate work area. Air-blowing spent metal polish near adjacent paint scatters it onto surfaces you don't want it on. - Do not rake the DA microfiber pad — air through the Tornador-style cleaner is enough.

## 13. Final Cleanup

After polishing is complete and the surface is wiped clean of all metal polish residue:
1. Multiple wipe-downs with cotton terry or clean microfiber until the wipe comes off clean. Metal polish residue is persistent — keep going.

2. If residue is still showing after the cotton terry / microfiber wipes, use the plush microfiber towel with whitening powder from the kit. The whitening powder pulls stubborn metal polish residue off the aluminum cleanly when cotton terry alone won't.
3. Final wipe with a clean multi-purpose 16"×16" (40×40 cm) microfiber for the mirror finish.
4. Remove the masking tape from the adjacent paint slowly at a low angle. Inspect for any polish that wicked under.
5. Remove all protective covering — plastic sheeting, contractor's paper, painter's paper, whichever was used — along with all securing tape.
6. Inspect the surrounding paint for any polish residue and wipe with a damp microfiber if found.

## 14. No Protection on Polished Brightwork

Polished brightwork is left bare. No Air Guard, no Static Guard, no ceramic coating, no sealant of any kind. Why:
- Coatings dull the mirror finish. Even the thinnest coating sits between the eye and the polished aluminum surface, scattering reflected light and reducing the chromed-mirror look that's the whole point of brightwork.
- Coatings yellow over time. UV exposure and heat cycle the coating, shifting it off-clear over months. The finish goes from polished aluminum to slightly amber polished aluminum.
- Coatings have to be fully removed before re-polishing. Adding work to the next service without preserving the look in between.
- Polished aluminum re-oxidizes regardless. The polishing cycle is part of brightwork maintenance — coatings don't stop oxidation, they just slow the visual decline of a finish that was supposed to look great in the first place. The Shiny Jets standard: brightwork is delivered polished, bare, mirror-finished. Customers who want lower maintenance can have their brightwork painted (that's a different conversation with a paint shop). Customers who want the polished look accept the maintenance cycle.

## 15. Quality Control

Walk each brightwork section under good light from multiple angles:
- Mirror finish — no swirls, holograms, haze, or visible polish lines
- No streaks from wipe-off
- No metal polish residue on adjacent paint
- All tape removed cleanly
- All protective covering removed (plastic, contractor's paper, painter's paper — whatever was used)
- Pitot tubes, static ports, vents, and joins free of metal polish or pad debris
- All tools, pads, towels, ladders, and creepers removed

Critical Don'ts
- Don't start at a higher level than needed. The least aggressive level that cuts the defect is the right level. Cutting more aggressively than needed wastes time — every level up adds refinement steps on the way back down.
- Don't skip taping the paint. Always tape paint adjacent to brightwork. Always.
- Don't let tape cover the brightwork itself. The tape line stops at the brightwork edge — tape on top of the polish surface lifts during work and leaves an uneven cutline.
- Don't leave paint exposed next to the brightwork. The tape covers the paint right up to the brightwork edge.
- Don't skip the alcohol pre-wipe before taping. Clean paint = tape adheres = no polish wicks under.
- Don't skip covering lenses and windows. Metal polish on a lens is hard to remove cleanly.
- Don't skip the plastic sheeting. Metal polish flings further than expected.
- Don't press hard. Pressure causes uneven cut and adds heat. Light pressure at every level.
- Don't skip the Maverick add-in with heavy metal polishes. Pad caking with caked NuVite or Top Cut causes uneven cut and pad damage.
- Don't skip back-down refinement on hard aluminum. Hard alloys need each step of the ladder on the way down. Soft alloys can sometimes jump to the Ryobi but only if the finish confirms it.
- Don't work in direct sunlight.
- Don't let the rotary dwell on a single spot. Continuous motion only.
- Don't clean pads near adjacent paint. Step away from the surface and clean.
- Don't put solution in the Tornador-style pad cleaner. Straight air only.
- Don't approach the aircraft interior after brightwork polishing without changing or covering clothing. Metal polish residue transfers permanently into leather and carpet.
